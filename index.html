    Introduction

    How to use this book

    This book is for people who already catch a third to half of what they hear and want the rest, fast. You don't need another course that starts with colours and greetings. You need the verb system to stop being a blur, and you need the words people actually say.

    Each study session has three steps, about 25 minutes in all:

    1. Read one chapter (10 min). Read the Spanish examples out loud. Do the Check yourself questions at the end before opening the answers.
    2. Look at the verb sheet for the tense you just read (5 min). Red forms break the regular pattern. Say a few rows aloud.
    3. Drill (10 min). Five minutes of Recognize (hear-it, know-it) and five of Produce (type it). The drill keeps track of what you miss and brings it back sooner.

    Why both drills. Recognition is what lets you follow a conversation: when you hear supieron you need to know instantly that it means "they found out." Production is what lets you answer. Most of your gap is probably recognition, so don't skip it.

    Suggested order. Week 1: chapters 1–2 and 10. Week 2: chapters 3–5. Week 3: chapters 6–7 and 11. Week 4: chapters 8–9 and 12. After that, keep drilling daily and reread a chapter whenever a tense feels shaky. Short daily sessions beat long weekend ones because spaced repetition only works if you come back.

    About the Spanish here. It is Latin American: ustedes for every plural "you," and no vosotros. Chapter 11 covers how coastal and Caribbean speech (including Panama) differs from the textbook, and chapter 12 covers the differences from Spain and from the vos countries.

    Chapter 1

    The present tense

    The present does more work in Spanish than in English. Hablo is "I speak," "I'm speaking," and often "I'll speak" (Mañana te llamo — I'll call you tomorrow). It also makes offers: ¿Te ayudo? — Shall I help you?

    Three present-tense patterns worth stealing

    • ir a + infinitive is the everyday future. Voy a firmar el lunes. — I'm going to sign on Monday.
    • acabar de + infinitive = "to have just." Acabo de llegar. — I just arrived.
    • hace + time + que + present = "have been doing for." Hace dos años que vivo aquí. — I've lived here for two years.

    Chapter 2

    Ser, estar and hay

    Both mean "to be." The rule of thumb: ser says what something is; estar says how or where it is right now or as a result of something.

    SerEstar
    identity, profession: Es abogado.location: El terreno está cerca del río.
    origin: Somos de Florida.condition: Estoy cansado.
    traits: La casa es grande.result of a change: La puerta está cerrada.
    time and dates: Son las tres.-ing forms: Estamos negociando.
    where an event takes place: La reunión es en la notaría.opinion of a specific instance: ¡La comida está rica!

    Same adjective, different meaning

    with serwith estar
    listocleverready
    aburridoboringbored
    ricorichdelicious
    malobad (character)sick / gone bad
    segurosafesure, certain

    Hay: there is, there are

    Hay comes from haber and never changes for singular or plural: hay un problema, hay dos problemas. In other tenses: había (there was / were, background), hubo (there was, an event), habrá (there will be), habría (there would be). Hay que + infinitive means "one has to": Hay que pagar el saldo.

    The meeting ___ at ten.es (time of an event)
    We ___ ready to sign.estamos listos
    The plot ___ very flat.es (a trait of the land)

    Chapter 3

    The preterite: what happened

    The preterite reports completed events: I signed, she called, they paid. In Latin American speech it also covers most of what English says with "have done": ¿Ya comiste? — Have you eaten yet?

    Regular endings

    -ar (hablar)-er / -ir (comer, vivir)
    yohablécomí, viví
    túhablastecomiste, viviste
    él / ustedhablócomió, vivió
    nosotroshablamoscomimos, vivimos
    ellos / ustedeshablaroncomieron, vivieron

    Listening tip. The stress on the last syllable is the giveaway: habló (he spoke) vs. hablo (I speak). Train your ear for that final stressed -ó.

    Small regular changes

    • Spelling, yo only: -car → qué (busqué, saqué, toqué), -gar → gué (llegué, pagué, jugué), -zar → cé (empecé, alcancé).
    • -ir stem changers change in él and ellos: pidió, pidieron; durmió, durmieron; sintió; siguió; murió.
    • i → y between vowels: leyó, creyó, oyó, cayó, construyó.

    The irregular "strong" preterites

    These share one set of endings, with no written accents: -e, -iste, -o, -imos, -ieron. Learn the stem and the whole tense follows.

    VerbStemyo / élVerbStemyo / él

    J-stems drop the i in the ellos form: dije … dijeron; traje … trajeron; conduje … condujeron; traduje … tradujeron; produje … produjeron.

    Four oddballs: ser and ir share one preterite (fui, fuiste, fue, fuimos, fueron; context tells you which). Dar: di, diste, dio, dimos, dieron. Ver: vi, viste, vio, vimos, vieron.

    Chapter 4

    The imperfect, and choosing between the two pasts

    The imperfect describes the past as an ongoing scene: what was happening, what used to happen, how things were. It is the easiest tense in Spanish.

    -ar (hablar)-er / -ir (comer, vivir)
    yohablabacomía
    túhablabascomías
    él / ustedhablabacomía
    nosotroshablábamoscomíamos
    ellos / ustedeshablabancomían

    Only three irregulars: ser (era, eras, era, éramos, eran), ir (iba, ibas, iba, íbamos, iban), ver (veía…).

    The film-set rule

    Think of a scene in a film. The imperfect is the set and the background action: the weather, the time, what people were doing, how they felt. The preterite is what happens on screen: the events that move the story forward.

    Eran las cinco y llovía. Estábamos en la oficina del abogado y esperábamos los papeles. De repente sonó el teléfono. Carlos dijo que no podía venir.

    It was five and it was raining (scene). We were at the lawyer's office waiting (scene). Suddenly the phone rang (event). Carlos said (event) he couldn't come (his situation: imperfect).

    ImperfectPreterite
    background, descriptionevents, completed actions
    habits: "used to," "would"a specific number of times
    time and age: Tenía 30 años.a sequence: first, then, after
    signals: siempre, cada día, de niño, mientras, normalmentesignals: ayer, de repente, una vez, el lunes, anoche

    Listening tip. -aba and -ía mean scene-setting. When you hear them, the speaker is giving context; the point of the story is coming in the preterite.

    Check yourself: imperfect or preterite?

    When I was young, I lived (vivir) in Ohio.vivía (background)
    Yesterday I signed (firmar) the contract.firmé (event)
    We were talking (hablar) when he arrived (llegar).hablábamos … llegó
    Every summer they went (ir) to Peru.iban (habit)

    The perfect tenses: haber + participle

    Exactly as in English: "I have spoken" = he hablado; "I had spoken" = había hablado. The participle never changes.

    Present perfectPluperfect
    yohehabía
    túhashabías
    él / ustedhahabía
    nosotroshemoshabíamos
    ellos / ustedeshanhabían
    + -ado (-ar verbs) or -ido (-er, -ir): hablado, comido, vivido

    Irregular participles

    abierto (abrir), dicho (decir), escrito (escribir), hecho (hacer), muerto (morir), puesto (poner), roto (romper), visto (ver), vuelto (volver), cubierto (cubrir), descubierto (descubrir), resuelto (resolver), supuesto (suponer). Compounds follow their base: devuelto, propuesto, deshecho.

    Regional note. Spain uses the present perfect for anything today. Most of Latin America prefers the preterite: Hoy hablé con él rather than Hoy he hablado con él. You'll hear the perfect most for life experience: ¿Has estado en Bocas?

    Participles also work as adjectives and agree: la puerta está abierta, los papeles están firmados.

    How they are really used

    • For plain future plans, speech mostly uses ir a: Vamos a ver. The future tense sounds more formal or more certain: Te lo enviaré mañana.
    • Future of probability — very common, often confusing: ¿Dónde estará Ana? = I wonder where Ana is. Serán las diez. = It's probably ten.
    • The conditional is the polite form: ¿Podría ayudarme? Could you help me? Me gustaría ver el título. I'd like to see the title.
    • Conditional of probability in the past: Serían las diez cuando llegó. It must have been about ten when he arrived.

    Chapter 7

    The present subjunctive

    The subjunctive is a verb form for things that aren't stated as fact: wishes, requests, feelings, doubts, and events that haven't happened yet. You can't avoid it. It's in que te vaya bien, cuando puedas, como quieras, and every polite request.

    How to form it: take yo, swap the vowel

    1. Start from the yo present: tengo, hablo, pido, conozco.
    2. Drop the -o.
    3. -ar verbs take -e endings; -er and -ir verbs take -a endings.
    hablartenerpedir
    yohabletengapida
    túhablestengaspidas
    él / ustedhabletengapida
    nosotroshablemostengamospidamos
    ellos / ustedeshablentenganpidan

    Because it starts from yo, all the -go and -zco verbs carry over: haga, diga, salga, ponga, venga, conozca, conduzca. -ir stem changers also change in nosotros: pidamos, durmamos, sintamos.

    Six true irregulars: dar → dé, ir → vaya, ser → sea, haber → haya, estar → esté, saber → sepa.

    When to use it

    The structure is almost always: trigger + que + different subject + subjunctive. Quiero que vengas. I want you to come. With the same subject you use the infinitive: Quiero venir.

    Trigger typeExamples
    Wanting, hopingquiero que, espero que, ojalá (que), prefiero que
    Asking, advisingte pido que, le recomiendo que, es mejor que, necesito que
    Feelingsme alegra que, siento que, me preocupa que, qué bueno que
    Doubt, denialdudo que, no creo que, no es cierto que, es posible que
    Necessityes importante que, es necesario que, hace falta que
    Purpose, not-yetpara que, antes de que, sin que, hasta que, en cuanto, cuando (future)

    The cuando trap. Cuando llego, te llamo is a habit (whenever I arrive). Cuando llegue, te llamo is a single future event (when I get there). In conversation about plans you'll almost always hear the subjunctive.

    Ready-made chunks

    Que te vaya bien (hope it goes well) · Cuando puedas (whenever you can) · Como quieras (as you like) · Lo que sea (whatever) · Ojalá que sí (I hope so) · No creo que sea así (I don't think that's how it is) · Avísame cuando sepas algo (let me know when you hear something).

    Check yourself

    It's important that we sign (firmar) today.Es importante que firmemos hoy.
    Call me when you know (saber).Llámame cuando sepas.

    Chapter 8

    The imperfect subjunctive and "if" sentences

    Formation: from the ellos preterite

    Take the ellos preterite, drop -ron, add -ra, -ras, -ra, -ramos, -ran (with an accent on the nosotros form). Every irregular preterite carries over automatically.

    Infinitiveellos preteriteImperfect subjunctive
    hablarhablaronhablara, hablaras, hablara, habláramos, hablaran
    tenertuvierontuviera, tuvieras, tuviera, tuviéramos, tuvieran
    ser / irfueronfuera, fueras …
    decirdijerondijera, dijeras …
    pedirpidieronpidiera, pidieras …

    There's a second form in -se (tuviese, hablase) used mostly in Spain and in writing. Recognize it; don't bother producing it.

    Three uses

    • A subjunctive trigger in the past: Quería que vinieras. I wanted you to come. Me pidió que firmara. He asked me to sign.
    • Extra polite: Quisiera hablar con el abogado. I would like to speak with the lawyer.
    • Hypothetical "if": see below.

    The three "if" patterns

    Typesi + …resultExample
    Realpresentpresent / future / commandSi tengo tiempo, voy.
    Hypotheticalimperfect subjunctiveconditionalSi tuviera tiempo, iría.
    Past unrealpluperfect subjunctive (hubiera + participle)habría + participleSi hubiera sabido, habría venido.

    In casual speech you'll often hear hubiera on both sides: Si hubiera sabido, hubiera venido. Both are correct.

    Como si (as if) always takes the imperfect subjunctive: Habla como si fuera el dueño. He talks as if he were the owner.

    Chapter 9

    Commands

    Tú, affirmative: use the él form

    Habla. Come. Escribe. Pide. Duerme. Eight short irregulars:

    decir → dihacer → hazir → veponer → pon
    salir → salser → sétener → tenvenir → ven

    Everything else: the subjunctive

    Usted, ustedes, nosotros ("let's"), and all negative commands are just present subjunctive forms.

    affirmativenegative
    túfirmano firmes
    ustedfirmeno firme
    nosotrosfirmemos (let's sign)no firmemos
    ustedesfirmenno firmen

    Vamos is the normal way to say "let's go." Vamos a + infinitive also means "let's": Vamos a ver.

    Where the pronouns go

    Affirmative commands attach pronouns to the end (add an accent to keep the stress): dímelo, mándemelo, siéntese. Negative commands put them in front: no me lo digas, no se siente.

    Usted in practice. Many speakers in Central America and Colombia use usted far more than in Spain or Mexico, including with friends and family. When someone addresses you as usted, answer the same way.

    The little words: object pronouns

    Much of what makes fast Spanish hard to follow is the string of one-syllable pronouns in front of the verb: se lo dije, me la mandó. Once these are automatic, comprehension jumps.

    Direct (it, him, her)Indirect (to / for someone)Reflexive
    yomememe
    tútetete
    él / ella / ustedlo / lalese
    nosotrosnosnosnos
    ellos / ustedeslos / laslesse
    • Order: indirect before direct. Me lo dio. He gave it to me.
    • le / les + lo / la becomes se: Se lo di. I gave it to him / her / you / them. Context or a él, a usted clears it up.
    • Placement: before a conjugated verb (lo quiero), or attached to an infinitive, gerund, or affirmative command (quiero verlo, estoy haciéndolo, hazlo).

    Gustar and its family

    These verbs work backwards: the thing is the subject. Me gusta el terreno. Me gustan los dos terrenos. Same pattern: me encanta (I love), me importa (it matters to me), me falta (I'm missing), me queda (I have left / it fits me), me duele (it hurts), me parece (it seems to me), me conviene (it suits me).

    Three uses of se you'll hear constantly

    • Impersonal / passive: Se vende lote. Lot for sale. ¿Cómo se dice…? How do you say…?
    • Accidents (it happened to me): Se me olvidó. I forgot. Se me cayó el teléfono. I dropped my phone. Se nos acabó el agua. We ran out of water.
    • Reflexive / change of state: Me levanto. Se fue. (He left.) Se puso nervioso. (He got nervous.)

    Check yourself

    Translate: ¿Me lo puede mandar?Can you send it to me?
    I already told him (it).Ya se lo dije.
    Translate: Se me olvidaron los papeles.I forgot the papers.
    Chapter 11

    Hearing real Spanish

    Textbook Spanish and street Spanish are the same language with different sound rules. Knowing the rules turns noise into words.

    Sound changes on the coasts and in the Caribbean (including Panama)

    • S softens to h, or disappears at the end of a syllable: ¿Cómo ehtáh? for ¿Cómo estás?; lah cosa for las cosas. Plural and tú endings vanish, so you rely on context and articles.
    • D between vowels drops: cansao (cansado), to' (todo), na' (nada).
    • Words fuse: pa' (para), pa'l (para el), p'acá (para acá), quiubo (¿qué hubo?).
    • R and L can swap at the end of a syllable in some Caribbean speech: puelta for puerta.

    Fillers and connectors

    These carry little meaning but signal where a sentence is going. Recognizing them frees attention for the content words.

    Chapter 12

    Latin America vs. Spain

    The grammar is the same everywhere. The differences are in how people say "you," a handful of tense habits, pronunciation, and everyday vocabulary. This book teaches Latin American usage; this chapter is so that Spanish TV, a podcast from Madrid, or a visitor from Buenos Aires doesn't throw you.

    "You" in the plural: ustedes vs. vosotros

    All of Latin America uses ustedes for any group of people. Spain uses vosotros for a group of friends and keeps ustedes for formal situations. You never need to produce vosotros forms, but you should recognize them:

    TenseSpain (vosotros)Latin America (ustedes)
    Presenthabláis, coméis, vivíshablan, comen, viven
    Preteritehablasteis, comisteishablaron, comieron
    Imperfecthablabais, comíaishablaban, comían
    Futurehablaréishablarán
    Subjunctivehabléis, comáishablen, coman
    Command¡hablad! ¡venid! ¡sentaos!¡hablen! ¡vengan! ¡siéntense!
    Pronounos (os lo digo)les / se (se lo digo)

    The giveaway is the sound -áis / -éis and the pronoun os.

    "You" in the singular: vos

    In Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay, most of Central America (Costa Rica, Nicaragua, Honduras, El Salvador, Guatemala) and parts of Colombia, many people say vos instead of tú. It changes the stress of the present and the command, and almost nothing else.

    túvos
    Presenthablas, tienes, puedes, ereshablás, tenés, podés, sos
    Commandhabla, ven, dihablá, vení, decí
    Other tensesusually the same as tú: tuviste, tenías, tendrás

    Note that vos present forms have no stem change: podés, not puedes; querés, not quieres. Object and possessive forms stay the tú ones: vos te llamás, ¿es tu casa?

    Tense habits

    • Recent past. Spain: Hoy he hablado con él. Latin America: Hoy hablé con él. (See chapter 5.)
    • Ahorita and other diminutives (cafecito, un ratito, cerquita) are everywhere in Latin America and rare in Spain.
    • Usted is used more widely in much of Latin America, including in some families. Spain uses tú with almost everyone under retirement age.

    Pronunciation

    • Z and soft C. Most of Spain says gracias and cerveza with a "th" sound. All of Latin America uses an "s" sound. Same words, so don't let the lisp-like sound confuse you.
    • J and G. In Spain the j is a raspy throat sound; in the Caribbean and Central America it's often a soft breathy h.
    • LL and Y. In Argentina and Uruguay they sound like "sh" or "zh": calle ≈ "cashe," yo ≈ "sho."
    • Dropped s. Southern Spain (Andalusia, the Canary Islands) drops final s much as the Caribbean does (chapter 11).

    One word to handle with care. In Spain coger just means "to take" or "to catch" (coger el autobús). In Mexico, Argentina and much of Latin America it is vulgar. Use tomar or agarrar instead and you're safe everywhere.
